Anatomy of an equation

Chemists write reactions as equations so anyone, in any language, can see exactly what reacts and what forms.

From words to symbols

When magnesium burns, it reacts with oxygen to make magnesium oxide. You can write that as a word equation:

magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide

Or as a symbol equation, which uses chemical formulas and shows how many particles are involved:

2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s)

The substances on the left are the reactants. The substances on the right are the products. You read the arrow as "reacts to form" or "produces".

State symbols

State symbols in brackets tell you whether each substance is a solid, liquid, gas or dissolved in water. They are useful, but you will not always see them.

SymbolMeaningExample
(s)solidMg(s), a strip of magnesium
(l)liquidH2O(l), liquid water
(g)gasCO2(g), carbon dioxide
(aq)aqueous, dissolved in waterNaCl(aq), salt water

Subscripts and coefficients

This is the most important idea on this page. Both are numbers, but they do very different jobs.

A subscript is part of the substance's identity. Change H2O to H2O2 and you no longer have water. When you balance equations, you may only change coefficients.

Elements that come in pairs

Seven elements are diatomic. As elements on their own, their atoms are always found joined in pairs, so you write them with a subscript 2.

H2   O2   F2   Br2   I2   N2   Cl2

Memory trick: say "HOFBrINCl" out loud, like "hoff-brinkle". That is H, O, F, Br, I, N, Cl.

This only applies when the element is by itself. In a compound, the formula tells you how many atoms there are. Water is H2O, not H2O2, even though oxygen gas is O2.
