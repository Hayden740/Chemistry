Nothing is lost

Read each section, stop to think when asked, then answer the questions at the end.

A puzzle about burning

Think about a campfire. You start with a heavy log and finish with a small pile of light, grey ash. It really looks as if most of the wood has been destroyed.

For a long time, many scientists thought something like that was happening. A popular idea in the 1700s was that burning materials released an invisible substance called phlogiston, so they should always get lighter.

There was a problem, though. When some metals were heated strongly in air, they turned into a powder that was heavier than the metal they started with. If burning only ever released something, how could anything gain mass?

Stop and think: why might a metal get heavier when it is heated in air?

It must be joining with something that comes from the air. We now know that something is oxygen. The metal and oxygen combine to make a metal oxide, so the powder includes the mass of the oxygen as well as the metal.

Weighing everything

The French chemist Antoine Lavoisier (1743 to 1794) solved the puzzle by doing something that sounds simple: he weighed everything, very carefully, including the gases. He worked with Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, who recorded results, made detailed drawings of their equipment and translated scientific papers so they could keep up with research from other countries.

In one famous experiment, Lavoisier sealed some tin inside a glass flask and weighed the whole thing. He heated it until a powdery coating formed on the tin. When he weighed the sealed flask again, the total mass had not changed at all.

Then he opened the flask. Air rushed in, and the mass went up. Some of the air inside had been used up by the tin, which left room for fresh air to enter.

His conclusion was that matter is not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. It only changes form. If you trap everything and weigh it, the total stays the same.

Stop and think: why was it so important that the flask was sealed?

Sealing the flask meant no gas could get in or out. Anything that was used up or made had to stay on the balance, so Lavoisier could compare the total mass before and after fairly.

The law of conservation of mass

In a chemical reaction, the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products. Mass is not created or destroyed.

Lavoisier could measure that this law was true, but he could not fully explain why. The explanation came in the early 1800s with John Dalton's atomic theory.

All substances are made of atoms. During a chemical reaction, the bonds holding atoms together break, and the atoms join up in new arrangements. That makes new substances, but the atoms themselves stay the same. No atoms appear from nowhere and none vanish. Same atoms in, same atoms out, so the same mass.

Open and closed systems

If mass is always conserved, why did the campfire log seem to lose so much? The answer is that we were not weighing everything.

When wood burns in the open, most of its atoms end up in carbon dioxide and water vapour. These gases float away, so the ash left on the balance weighs less. The mass has not been destroyed. It has just gone somewhere the balance cannot measure.

The opposite can happen too. When steel wool burns in air, oxygen from the air joins onto the iron. The balance reading goes up, because it now includes atoms that came from the air.

Detective tip: if a balance reading changes during a reaction, look for a gas. Either a gas escaped, or a gas from the air joined in.

Stop and think: a student says "the mass went down, so conservation of mass is wrong". How would you reply?

Mass is still conserved, but the system was open. A gas product escaped into the air, so it was no longer being weighed. If the reaction was done in a sealed container, the reading would stay the same.
